# Wada colour combinations

Sanzo Wada (1883–1967) compiled *Haishoku Sōkan* (1933–34), six volumes of colour
combinations for designers, dyers and painters; Seigensha reissued it as *A Dictionary of
Color Combinations*. It holds 348 combinations of two, three or four colours, drawn from 159
named colours. They are tested pairings, not generated harmonies: a palette here is a choice
a practised colourist already made.

## Files

- `colors.json` — the 159 colours (name, CMYK as printed, Lab, sRGB, hex) and, for each, the
  numbers of the combinations it is in. From
  [mattdesl/dictionary-of-colour-combinations](https://github.com/mattdesl/dictionary-of-colour-combinations)
  (MIT, `LICENSE.md`), which converted the book's CMYK to sRGB through ICC profiles
  (U.S. Web Coated SWOP v2 to sRGB). Screen colours are a conversion of print colours:
  close, not exact.
- `combinations.md` — one line per combination, generated by `npm run sync`
  (`engine/palettes/wada.js`): the colours with hex, three computed tags, and the
  highest-contrast pair in it.

The tags are measurements from Lab, with hand-set thresholds, not Wada's own labels:

- **harmony** — `same-tone` when every hued colour sits within 45° of hue; `contrast-tone`
  when they spread further; `neutral-led` when fewer than two colours have any real hue.
- **value** — `light-and-dark` when lightness spans 40 or more; otherwise `light`, `mid`
  or `dark` by average lightness.
- **strength** — `soft` when no colour reaches chroma 25, `strong` when one passes 60,
  otherwise `moderate`.

## The method, as Wada describes it

His preface to the original volumes sets out how to use the combinations. In short, in our
words:

1. **Two families of harmony.** Same-tone combinations stay close in hue and build harmony
   from steps of light and dark; contrast-tone combinations join different hues and find
   harmony across the difference. Each family runs from light and pale to dark, deep and
   strong, plus combinations whose colours share an undertone. Same-tone is the safer
   choice for large surfaces; contrast-tone is harder to balance and more interesting when
   it works.
2. **Never equal amounts.** The book shows each combination as equal swatches only so they
   can be compared. In use, one colour takes most of the area, a second takes less and the
   third or fourth is a small accent; his example is a kimono, its sash and the collar
   lining. He suggests cutting the swatches into strips and trying different proportions
   before deciding.
3. **Area changes the colour.** A colour over a large area reads weaker and lighter than
   the small swatch; a small patch reads stronger. Choose the large-area colour with that
   in mind.
4. **The surface matters.** The same colour reads differently on silk and on cotton, glossy
   and matte, transparent and opaque. On screen: the same hex differs on a flat fill, under
   texture, and as thin type.

## Using one in a design

Pick by the brief: the mood (harmony, value, strength) and whether the combination has a
pair that can carry text. 213 of the 348 have no pair reaching 4.5:1, so most designs need
a text colour from outside the combination; declare it as an exception instead of hiding
it. Name the combination by number (`006`) wherever it is used, so it can be traced back.
